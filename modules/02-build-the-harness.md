# Module 02 — Build the Harness: Context Is Not a Prompt
### Hour 2 | Artifact: Personal Context Stack | ~60 min conversation

---

## TUTOR DELIVERY RULES
- ≤ 3 paragraphs per turn. Diagrams don't count as paragraphs.
- Every turn ends with a question or invitation.
- [CHECKPOINT] every 2–4 turns.
- This module sits at the SCAFFOLD node of the Semantifacturing loop.
- See `AGENTS.md` for the full contract.

---

## SEMANTIFACTURING LOCATION

Orient the learner at the start:

```
   INTENTION ──► SCAFFOLD ──► GENERATE ──► ASSESS
       ▲              ▲                      │
       └──────────────┼─── REFINE ◄──────────┘
                      │
               [ We are here — Module 2 ]
```

> In Module 1 you clarified your intention. Now we build the **scaffold** — the cognitive environment you construct *before* you ask your AI anything important. Most people skip this entirely. That is why most people feel like the AI is working on someone else's project.

**End with:**
> Quick question before we build anything: when you use your AI tool today, what do you typically include before your actual question? What does your setup look like?

---

## TURN 2 — Name the gap. One diagram.

*(Tutor: Receive their answer. If they say "nothing" or "just the question" — that is the gap. If they say "some context" — ask what kind. Then show the diagram.)*

**Deliver:**

```
HOW MOST PEOPLE USE AI:

  [Active Question]  ──►  AI  ──►  Use Answer
         ▲
    (starts here —
     everything above
     is left implicit)

─────────────────────────────────────────────

HOW A HARNESS WORKS:

  [1. Constitution]         ← your intent
  [2. Source Material]      ← your actual work
  [3. Current State]        ← where things are NOW
  [4. Operating Instructions] ← how AI should behave
  [5. Active Question]  ──►  AI  ──►  Your output
```

> The difference between these two workflows is not prompt quality. It is *what the AI is working inside of.* Without layers 1–4, the AI is drawing from its statistical training — its generic prior about questions like yours. With them, it is working inside your reality.

**End with:**
> Which of those four layers do you think would make the biggest difference for your specific project right now — and why?

---

## TURN 3 — Layer 2: Source Material. Where it comes from.

*(Tutor: Respond to what they said. Then address Layer 2 specifically. Which of these the learner can actually hand to the AI depends on their tool; `reference/your-tools.md` explains the differences. Do not name products.)*

**Deliver:**

```
LAYER 2 — SOURCE MATERIAL

Where it comes from:
  ┌──────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Your own drafts                  │ ← paste them in, or point
  │                                  │   the AI at the file
  │ Your notes                       │ ← the raw thinking, not
  │                                  │   the polished version
  │ Documents on your machine        │ ← if your tool reads files
  │ Pages on the web                 │ ← if your tool can fetch them
  │ Decisions you've already made    │ ← your current-projects file
  │ Feedback from real people        │ ← the world answering back
  └──────────────────────────────────┘
```

> Without source material, the AI produces plausible answers — not your answers. The model's priors are built from millions of documents that are not about your project. Your source material is the gravitational pull that keeps output relevant to your reality. Which of these your tool can actually reach depends on the tool; `reference/your-tools.md` tells you how to find out.

**End with:**
> For your specific project — what source material exists right now that the AI could actually read? A draft you've written, notes you've taken, a document, a page, a decision you've already made.

---

## [CHECKPOINT — Turn 3] Assessment 1

> Let's check in on your scaffolding instincts.
> 1. **Project clarity:** Has your one-sentence project description from Module 1 changed at all now that you've thought about what source material exists?
> 2. **Intentional refinement:** What would you include in Layer 3 (Current State) right now — what decisions have been made, what paths have been rejected?
> 3. **Prompt upgrade:** If you had to write a Layer 4 Operating Instruction for how you want the AI to behave on your project — one rule — what would it be?

*(Tutor: The Layer 4 question is the most revealing. "Don't change my argument while improving my writing" = they understand the distinction. "Be helpful" = they haven't thought about it yet. Work with whatever they give you.)*

---

## TURN 4 — Layer 4: Operating Instructions. This is the most underused layer.

**Deliver:**

```
LAYER 4 — OPERATING INSTRUCTIONS
(what most people never write)

  Examples that change what you get:
  ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
  "Preserve my existing thesis unless you find a
   specific contradiction — and name the contradiction."

  "When you introduce a new idea, label it [NEW]."

  "Distinguish critique from rewrite. Don't do
   both at once without asking first."

  "Do not agree with me reflexively. Tell me
   when you disagree and why."

  "If your answer depends primarily on assumptions,
   say so before you give it."
  ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
```

> These instructions do not make the AI smarter — they make it more specifically yours. They convert a general-purpose instrument into one calibrated for your project and your taste. The absence of these instructions is the most common reason people feel like the output belongs to someone else.

**End with:**
> Which of those examples resonates most for your project? And what rule would you add that isn't on that list?

---

## TURN 5 — The Live Comparison Exercise.

*(Tutor: This is the hands-on core of the module. Guide the learner through both rounds.)*

**Deliver:**

> Let's do this together right now, with your real project.

> **Round 1:** Open a new session with your AI tool, a fresh chat. Type only: *"Make this better: [paste one piece of your actual work — a draft, a description, an email]."* Save the result.

> **Round 2:** In the same conversation, build your five-layer harness — paste your Constitution, name your source material, describe your current state, write one Operating Instruction, then ask the same question.

**End with:**
> Read both outputs side by side. Tell me: which one felt like yours? Not which was better-written — which one felt like it came from inside your project rather than being applied to it from outside?

---

## [CHECKPOINT — Turn 5–6] Assessment 2

> Three questions before we save your context stack:
> 1. **Your work:** What was the most significant difference between Round 1 and Round 2? Was it the content, the tone, the relevance — or something harder to name?
> 2. **Recursive refinement:** Did building the harness for Round 2 surface anything about your project you didn't know you needed to articulate?
> 3. **Prompt efficiency:** Your Layer 4 Operating Instruction — is it specific enough that a stranger could follow it, or is it still too general?

*(Tutor: Push on question 3. "Be clear and concise" is too general. "Preserve my argument structure even if you rewrite the sentences" is specific. Help them reach specificity before saving. Keep their final instruction; it is recorded at the close.)*

## TURN 6 — What not to put in the harness. Safety, plainly.

*(Tutor: This turn is about operational safety, and it belongs here because Layer 2 is where people paste things they should not. Deliver it directly. No lecture; one diagram, three short paragraphs, one question.)*

**Deliver:**

```
WHAT THE AI CAN SEE                  WHAT NEVER GOES IN
─────────────────────────────────    ─────────────────────────────────
What you paste                       Other people's private details
The folder you opened, if your       Client, patient or student data
  tool reads files                   Passwords, keys, account numbers
A page you point it at, if it        Anything you signed a contract
  can fetch                            or a promise not to share
Nothing you have not handed it       Anything you would not want
  or connected: not your email,        read back in a meeting
  not your other documents,
  not your screen
```

> Source material is powerful because it is specific, and specific material is exactly the kind that can belong to someone else. The rule is simple: before anything goes into Layer 2, ask whose it is. Your own draft, your own notes, your own decisions: yours to paste. A customer's file, a colleague's message, a patient's record: not yours to paste, however useful it would be. Summarize what you need in your own words instead, with names and identifiers out.

> Then find one setting, today. Most tools have a switch that decides whether what you type is used to train future models. It usually lives under account, privacy, or data controls. If you cannot find it, assume the answer is yes and paste accordingly. The AI's memory of your work, where it has one, is a convenience for you and a liability for anyone else whose details end up in it.

**End with:**
> Two questions, both practical. What is one thing in your source material that you should never paste, and what would you paste instead? And do you know, right now, whether your tool trains on what you type?

## TURN 7 — Save the artifact.

*(Tutor: Compile the learner's five-layer harness into a reusable template with their actual content in each layer, following the shape of `workbook/artifacts/context-stack.md`. Read it back. Ask: "Does this feel complete? Anything to add before we save it?")*

Once confirmed, save it to `workbook/artifacts/context-stack.md`, replacing the template. If that file already holds learner content, copy it to `workbook/history/context-stack-YYYY-MM-DD-HHMM.md` first and say so.

> Your context stack is saved. Every new important session with your AI starts with this — paste Layers 1–4, then add your Layer 5 for that specific task. Two minutes of setup saves the entire session from drift.

> Module 3 is where we stop defending against drift and start using the AI offensively — generating multiple angles simultaneously, then synthesizing them yourself. Before we go there: what is the one real-world step you'll take on this project before we next meet?
